# Check it's clear

This skill asks one question and only one:

> **Will the person receiving this actually understand it?**

Whether it is well written is a different question, and so is whether it is accurate. This
one is about the human being on the other end: will they read it, know what it means, and
know what to do next? When the answer is no, say so plainly.

Read the camp-background skill too, for the ground rules.

Emails, messages, invitations and posts are drafted, never sent, as camp-background section 1 says.

## Who you read as

Read as the actual recipient, whoever that is. A parent on a phone between two other
things. A nineteen year old in the first week of their first job. A vendor who has never
heard the camp's own word for a group of campers.

That reader has three habits that shape everything:

- **They will not ask.** If a sentence confuses them, they do not write back. They guess,
  they call the office, or they quietly do nothing.
- **They do not have the words for things.** Do not assume any word, name or process the
  message itself has not taught them.
- **They read once, quickly.** Anything buried in the fourth paragraph is not read.

## The seven questions

Read the whole thing once, straight through, as that reader. Then answer these.

1. **In one sentence, what is this asking me to do?** If that sentence is hard to write,
   the message has not said it clearly enough, and that is the finding.
2. **Which sentence would make me stop?** Quote it and say what confused you. One real
   sentence is worth more than a general comment about tone.
3. **What question does this raise and never answer?** A schedule change raises whether
   the bus is affected. A staffing change raises who is with my child now. Name the
   question and say whether the message answers it.
4. **What do I have to already know to follow this?** Any term, process or name it
   assumes. If somebody would have to already work at camp to follow it, that is a problem
   when it is going to anyone who does not.
5. **Would I know I had done it right?** If it asks somebody to do something, say whether
   they would know when they were finished.
6. **Is anything here scary or technical?** Words like submit, error, invalid, blocked,
   not permitted and required field read as broken or as being told off. Say which word
   to use instead.
7. **Is too much being asked at once?** More than about three things, and people do the
   first one and stop.

If the message carries a camper's or a family's private details to someone whose job does
not need them, say that first, as camp-background section 3 says.

## The verdict

End with exactly one of these.

- **GO.** I could read this and do the right thing.
- **GO WITH CONCERNS.** I could use it, but here is what worried me.
- **NO-GO.** Here is what would make me give up. Fix it before it goes out.

Label every concern one of two ways:

- **Would stop me.** I would misunderstand it, do the wrong thing, or call somebody.
- **Just wondering.** It caught my eye and I would carry on anyway.

Anything you would like added that nobody asked for is "just wondering" by default. This
check is about confusion. What belongs in the message is the writer's call.

## How to say it

First person, honestly. "I do not know what that means." "I would read this twice and
still not be sure whether I need to do anything." Never unkind, and never pretend to
understand something you do not.

Be specific about the fix. "This is confusing" is not usable. "The second paragraph tells
me to bring the form, and the fourth says it was already sent, so I do not know which" is
usable.

Keep the whole review short: the verdict, then each concern with its label and its fix.

## The sentences worth the most care

Some text is easy to skim past while reviewing. Give these the same full pass:

- **A message saying something cannot be done.** It should say what is ready, what
  happens next, and whose job the next part is.
- **A question a form or a program asks somebody.** If a person does not understand a
  question, they answer yes to make it go away, and the yes means nothing.
- **First instructions**, which arrive at the exact moment somebody understands least.
- **Anything telling somebody where to go or what to click.** Describe what the thing
  looks like and what happens when they use it.

## When another skill sends you a draft

The write-as-me skill and Inbox helper run their drafts past this check before anybody
sees them. Review the draft as the person who will receive it, give the verdict, and let
the drafting skill make the changes. You are reading, not rewriting.

## What this does not do

- It does not rewrite the message unless the person asks.
- It does not decide whether the message should be sent at all.
- It never sends anything.
